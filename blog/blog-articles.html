How Much Does a Website Cost in Zimbabwe? (2026 Guide)

Short answer: the cost of a website in Zimbabwe depends on four things: how many pages you need, whether you take online payments (EcoCash, Paynow, Visa), whether you need an online shop, and who writes the content. On top of the build, plan for yearly running costs: your .co.zw domain, hosting and a business email. Always ask for a written, fixed quote that lists exactly what is included.

"How much is a website?" is the question we hear more than any other. It is also the question most web designers in Zimbabwe answer with "inbox for price". That leaves business owners guessing, comparing quotes that look nothing alike, and worrying they are being overcharged.

This guide explains what you are really paying for, so you can compare quotes with confidence.

Why website quotes in Zimbabwe vary so much

Ask five designers in Harare or Bulawayo for a price and you may get five very different numbers for what sounds like "the same website". Published local prices run from very cheap one-page packages to several thousand US dollars for larger builds. Both ends can be fair. They are simply different products.

A low quote usually means a template with your logo and text dropped in, few pages and no extras. A higher quote usually includes custom design, more pages, payments, a content manager, search engine optimisation (SEO) setup and support after launch. The problem is not the range. The problem is quotes that don't tell you which one you are getting.

The 4 things that change the price of a website

1. Number of pages

A one-page site (sometimes called a landing page) is the simplest and cheapest option. Most small businesses need more: a home page, about, services (often one page per service), a gallery or portfolio, and contact. A law firm, school or NGO might need twenty pages or more. More pages mean more design, more content and more testing.

Tip: list the pages you need before you ask for quotes. It is the fastest way to get prices you can compare.

2. Online payments: EcoCash, Paynow and cards

Do you want customers to pay on the website? Taking payments through a gateway such as Paynow lets customers pay with EcoCash, OneMoney, InnBucks, ZimSwitch and Visa or Mastercard. That means extra setup, testing and security work, so it adds to the cost. Payment gateways also charge their own transaction fees, separate from what you pay your designer.

If you only need people to contact you, you may not need online payments at all. A clear call and WhatsApp button can be enough.

3. An online shop (e-commerce)

An online shop is more than a payment button. It needs product pages, stock, a cart, delivery options, order emails and a way for your team to manage orders. It is one of the biggest jumps in price, and it is worth it when you sell many products to many customers, including the diaspora.

4. Who writes the content

Someone has to write the words and supply the photos. If you provide polished text and good images, the job is faster. If the agency writes your copy, takes product photos or optimises each page for Google, that is real work and it should be in the quote. Good content is what turns a website from a brochure into a sales tool.

Yearly costs to budget for (after the website is built)

Many business owners are surprised by bills that arrive a year later. Ask about these up front:

  • Domain name. Your .co.zw (or .com) address is renewed every year. In Zimbabwe, .co.zw domains are managed by ZISPA through accredited registrars; prices and verification times vary by registrar, so ask yours for the exact renewal fee.
  • Hosting. The server your website lives on. Cheap hosting can make a site slow on mobile data, which costs you visitors.
  • Business email. An address like info@yourbusiness.co.zw builds trust far more than a free email address.
  • Maintenance and updates. Security updates, backups and small content changes. Check whether these are included, billed per change or covered by a monthly plan.

Important: make sure the domain is registered in your name or your company's name, not the designer's. If you ever change providers, you need to be able to take your domain with you.

A simple checklist for comparing website quotes

Before you accept any quote, check that it answers these questions:

  1. How many pages are included, and which ones?
  2. Is the design custom or based on a template?
  3. Are online payments or a shop included? Which payment methods?
  4. Who writes the content and supplies photos?
  5. Is basic SEO included (page titles, descriptions, Google Business Profile, speed)?
  6. Will the site work well on phones and load quickly on mobile data?
  7. Can you update the site yourself, through a content manager?
  8. What are the yearly costs: domain, hosting, email and support?
  9. Whose name is the domain registered in?
  10. What happens after launch: who fixes problems, and how fast?

If a quote can't answer these in writing, ask before you pay a deposit.

Cheap vs professional: which do you need?

A cheap website is not always a bad choice. If you are just starting out and mainly need somewhere to send people from WhatsApp or Facebook, a simple, fast one-page site with your services and a WhatsApp button can be a smart first step.

You need more when your website has to win customers on its own: when people find you on Google, compare you with competitors, and decide without speaking to you first. That is when design, content, speed and SEO start paying for themselves.

The expensive mistake is paying for a website that looks fine but does not work on a phone, can't be found on Google, or can't be updated without paying the designer every time.

5 Signs You're Overpaying for Your Website (and What to Do)

Short answer: you are probably overpaying for your website if (1) the domain isn't registered in your name, (2) renewal fees arrive that you never agreed to, (3) there was never a written quote, (4) every small update costs extra, or (5) the site is slow on mobile data. Each one is fixable, and most can be fixed without rebuilding the site.

Price alone doesn't tell you whether a website is good value. A cheap site that loses customers is expensive, and an expensive site with hidden extras may still be a bad deal. Here are the five warning signs we see most often when reviewing websites for Zimbabwean businesses, with what to do about each.

1. The domain isn't in your name

Your domain name (for example yourbusiness.co.zw) is your address online. It is on your business cards, your vehicles and your WhatsApp profile. If your web designer registered it in their name, they effectively control it. If you fall out, stop paying or simply want to move providers, getting it back can be slow, stressful or costly.

How to check: look up your domain's registration details with your registrar, or ask your designer for the registrant name in writing. For .co.zw domains, the registrant's contact details are public, which makes this easy to verify.

What to do: ask for the domain to be transferred into your name or your company's name. A trustworthy provider will do this without argument. Going forward, make "the domain is registered to us" a condition of any website contract.

2. Surprise renewal fees

A year after launch, an invoice arrives for "annual renewal" that is far higher than you expected, or that you never knew about. Sometimes it bundles the domain, hosting, email and "maintenance" into one number with no breakdown.

Yearly costs are normal: domains, hosting and email all renew every year. Surprise is not normal.

What to do: ask for a breakdown of every recurring cost, with what it covers and when it is due. Compare the hosting and email prices with what other providers charge for the same service. If you are paying for "maintenance", ask what was actually done this year: updates, backups and security checks should be reported, not assumed.

3. There was never a written quote

If your website was agreed on a phone call or a few WhatsApp messages, it is very hard to know what you paid for, and very easy for costs to grow. Many disputes between businesses and web designers in Zimbabwe start here.

A written quote protects both sides. It should list:

  • the pages and features included
  • the design approach (template or custom)
  • who supplies the content and photos
  • the timeline and payment schedule
  • the yearly costs after launch
  • what support you get after launch, and for how long

What to do: for your current site, ask your provider to put your existing arrangement in writing. For your next project, never pay a deposit without one.

4. You pay for every small update

Changing a phone number, adding a new product or posting a news update shouldn't need a developer, and it shouldn't cost you every time. If each small change means an invoice and a wait of several days, your website gets out of date and stops working for you.

Modern websites can include a content manager: a simple admin area where your team logs in to edit text, add photos and publish articles, much like posting on social media.

What to do: ask whether your site has (or can have) a content manager and training for your team. Agree on which changes you'll make yourself and which are covered by a support plan. For bigger changes, agree a price list up front.

5. It's slow on mobile data

Most people in Zimbabwe browse the internet on their phones, often on mobile data that is expensive and sometimes slow. A website full of huge images and heavy scripts eats your customers' data and takes so long to load that many leave before it appears. You paid for a website; they never saw it.

How to check: open your website on your phone using mobile data, not Wi-Fi. Does it load in a few seconds? Is the text readable without zooming? Can you find your phone number and a WhatsApp button straight away? Free tools such as Google PageSpeed Insights also give you a speed score and a list of fixes.

What to do: ask your provider to compress images, remove unused plugins and test on mobile. Speed is one of the cheapest improvements with the biggest payoff.

Bonus: you can't measure what the website does

If nobody can tell you how many people visit your site, where they come from or how many contact you, you can't know whether it is worth what you pay. Basic analytics and a contact form or WhatsApp click count should be part of every business website.

What a clear renewal invoice looks like

A fair yearly invoice is easy to read. Each recurring cost is on its own line, with what it covers and the period it pays for:

  • Domain renewal: yourbusiness.co.zw, 12 months, with the renewal date
  • Hosting: the plan name, storage and the period covered
  • Business email: the number of mailboxes
  • Support plan (if you have one): what's included, such as monthly updates, backups, security checks and a set number of content changes

If your invoice says only "Annual website fee" with one number, ask for this breakdown. You're entitled to know what you're paying for, and it makes comparing providers simple.

A quick self-audit you can do in 15 minutes

  1. Look up who the domain is registered to.
  2. Find your last two website invoices and list every recurring item.
  3. Open your site on your phone using mobile data and time how long it takes to load.
  4. Try to change one small thing yourself. Is there a login, and does it work?
  5. Ask your provider for a copy of your agreement.

If two or more of these steps raise problems, it's worth getting a second opinion before your next renewal.

What a fair website deal looks like

You are getting good value when:

  • the domain is in your name
  • the price and yearly costs are written down
  • you can make simple updates yourself
  • the site is fast and easy to use on a phone
  • you know who to call when something breaks, and how quickly they'll respond

How to Accept EcoCash and Paynow Payments on Your Website

Short answer: most Zimbabwean businesses accept online payments by connecting their website to a payment gateway such as Paynow. One integration lets customers pay with EcoCash, OneMoney, InnBucks, ZimSwitch and Visa or Mastercard. You open a merchant account, verify your business, connect the gateway to your website or online shop, and test it before going live. The gateway charges a fee per transaction and pays the money into your bank account.

Taking payments on your website means you can sell while you sleep, take deposits for bookings, and sell to customers in the diaspora. Here's how it works in Zimbabwe, and what to watch out for.

Why take payments on your website?

  • Fewer lost sales. Customers who are ready to buy can pay immediately, instead of waiting for you to reply with bank details.
  • Less admin. Payments are matched to orders automatically, so there's no need to chase screenshots of proof of payment.
  • Diaspora customers. Family abroad can pay by card for goods or services delivered in Zimbabwe.
  • Trust. A proper checkout looks professional and reassures first-time buyers.

You don't need a full online shop to benefit. Many service businesses use a simple "pay a deposit" or "pay this invoice" page.

What is Paynow?

Paynow is a Zimbabwean payment gateway. Instead of integrating each payment method separately, your website connects to Paynow once, and Paynow handles the different methods. According to Paynow's developer documentation, these include Visa, Mastercard, EcoCash, OneMoney, InnBucks, ZimSwitch, Omari and others.

Other gateways and bank merchant services exist in Zimbabwe, and some banks offer their own card acquiring. Paynow is a common starting point for small and medium businesses because of its range of local payment methods.

Step by step: adding EcoCash and Paynow to your website

1. Decide what you're selling and how

Will customers buy products from a shop, pay a deposit for a booking, or settle an invoice? This decides whether you need a full e-commerce setup or a simpler payment page.

2. Open a merchant account

Register your business with the payment gateway and complete its verification. Expect to provide business registration documents, ID for the directors and bank account details. Card payments (Visa and Mastercard) usually need a verified account, so start early because verification can take time.

3. Connect the gateway to your website

Your developer connects the website to the gateway using its integration tools (Paynow provides official libraries for common programming languages). For WordPress and WooCommerce sites, plugins are often available. The website sends the order amount to the gateway, the customer chooses how to pay, and the gateway tells your website whether the payment succeeded.

For card payments, customers are often redirected to the gateway's secure page to enter their card details, then sent back to your site. That's normal, and it means your website never handles card numbers directly.

4. Set up order confirmations

When a payment succeeds, your website should automatically: - mark the order as paid - show the customer a thank-you page - send a confirmation by email or SMS - notify your team (many businesses also send a WhatsApp alert to staff)

5. Test everything before going live

Make test payments with each method: EcoCash, OneMoney, card. Check what happens when a payment fails or is cancelled, and that the money arrives in your bank account as expected.

6. Go live and tell your customers

Add "Pay with EcoCash, OneMoney or Visa" badges to your website and social media. People buy more readily when they see their preferred way to pay.

How much does it cost to accept payments online?

There are two kinds of costs:

  1. Setup: your developer's time to integrate and test the gateway, which is part of your website quote.
  2. Transaction fees: the gateway charges a percentage and/or a fixed fee per payment, and rates often differ between mobile money and international cards. Check the gateway's current fee page before you set your prices, and decide whether you'll absorb the fee or include it in your prices.

Also ask how and when funds are settled. Card and mobile money payments may settle into different accounts (USD or ZiG) on different schedules.

Pay online or pay on delivery?

Not every business needs to take every payment online. A common approach in Zimbabwe is to offer both:

  • Pay online for fixed-price products, deposits and bookings. It locks in the sale and reduces no-shows.
  • Pay on delivery or collection for customers who prefer to see the goods first, or for large custom orders confirmed on WhatsApp.

Make the options clear at checkout. Offering a small benefit for paying online, such as priority delivery or a confirmed booking slot, nudges customers towards the faster, cleaner option without forcing them.

Selling to the diaspora

Zimbabweans abroad regularly pay for goods and services for family at home: groceries, building materials, school fees, gifts and services. A website that accepts Visa and Mastercard opens this market to you. To win diaspora buyers:

  • show prices in US dollars
  • explain delivery areas and timeframes clearly
  • send photo or WhatsApp confirmation when the order is delivered
  • include reviews from other diaspora customers

These small trust signals matter more when the buyer can't visit your shop.

Common pitfalls to avoid

  • Leaving verification too late. Start the merchant application while your website is being built.
  • Not handling failed payments. Customers whose payment failed need a clear message and a way to try again.
  • Hiding the total cost. Show delivery and any fees before the customer pays.
  • No receipts. Always send an automatic confirmation; it reduces "did my payment go through?" messages.
  • Ignoring data protection. Your checkout collects names, phone numbers and emails, which is personal data. See Is your website POTRAZ-ready?.

Can I sell on WhatsApp and still take online payments?

Yes, and it's often the best setup. Customers browse your products and prices on your website, then either pay online or tap "Order on WhatsApp" to ask questions before paying. Read Website + WhatsApp: how they work together.

Website + WhatsApp: How They Work Together for Zimbabwean Businesses

Short answer: you don't have to choose between WhatsApp and a website. The website helps new customers find you on Google and answers their questions: prices, products, location, reviews. WhatsApp is where you have the conversation and close the sale. Connect them with "Order on WhatsApp" buttons, a WhatsApp Business catalogue and online payments, and each one makes the other work better.

In Zimbabwe, WhatsApp is where business happens. Customers message to ask prices, place orders and send proof of payment. So it's fair to ask: "My customers are all on WhatsApp — why do I need a website?"

Here's the honest answer, and how to set up both so they work together.

What WhatsApp does well and where it falls short

WhatsApp is great for: - personal, fast conversations - answering specific questions - confirming orders and deliveries - repeat customers who already have your number

But WhatsApp can't: - help new customers find you when they search on Google - show your full range, prices and policies in a way people can browse at 10 pm without messaging you - build trust with someone who has never heard of you - take card payments from the diaspora on its own

If every new customer has to message you just to see your prices, you lose the ones who don't want to ask, and you spend hours answering the same questions.

What a website adds

A website is the part of your business that works while you're busy:

  • It gets you found. When someone searches "furniture shop Harare" or "plumber near me", Google shows websites and Google Business Profiles, not WhatsApp chats.
  • It answers questions in advance. Prices, product photos, delivery areas, opening hours and FAQs mean fewer repetitive messages.
  • It builds trust. A professional site with real photos, reviews and contact details reassures first-time buyers.
  • It can take payments. EcoCash, OneMoney, InnBucks, ZimSwitch and Visa through a payment gateway such as Paynow.

How they work together: a simple customer journey

  1. Found on Google. A customer searches for what you sell and finds your website or Google Business Profile.
  2. Browse on your website. They see your products, prices and delivery details.
  3. Tap "Order on WhatsApp". A button on every product page opens a WhatsApp chat with a pre-filled message ("Hi, I'd like the teak coffee table").
  4. Close the sale on WhatsApp. You answer final questions, confirm stock and arrange delivery.
  5. Pay. The customer pays online by EcoCash or card, or through your usual method.

The website did the selling; WhatsApp closed the deal. And your team only spends time on customers who are already interested.

How to set it up

Add "Order on WhatsApp" and "Chat with us" buttons

Every product and service page should have a clear WhatsApp button. Use a click-to-chat link (wa.me/ followed by your number in international format) with a pre-filled message that includes the product name, so you know exactly what they're asking about.

Use a WhatsApp Business account

WhatsApp Business gives you a business profile, quick replies for common questions, labels to organise chats and a product catalogue. Add your website address to your WhatsApp Business profile so people can browse more.

Keep your catalogue and website in sync

Your WhatsApp catalogue is a handy shop window inside the app. Your website is the full store that Google can find. Keep prices and stock consistent across both.

Add online payments where it makes sense

For products with fixed prices, let customers pay online. For custom work, take a deposit online after the WhatsApp conversation. See How to accept EcoCash and Paynow on your website.

Measure what works

Track how many people click your WhatsApp buttons from the website. It's the simplest way to see your website turning visitors into conversations.

Example: how a furniture shop in Harare could set this up

Imagine a furniture maker in Harare that currently sells only through WhatsApp and a Facebook page.

  1. Website: a simple site with a page for each product range (tables, chairs, beds), real photos, prices or "from" prices, delivery areas and a clear "Order on WhatsApp" button on every product.
  2. Google Business Profile: listed as a furniture store, with the showroom on Google Maps, opening hours, photos and reviews.
  3. WhatsApp Business: quick replies for delivery costs, lead times and payment options, plus a catalogue matching the website.
  4. Payments: deposits taken online by EcoCash or card for custom orders; the balance paid on delivery.

Now a customer searching "dining table Harare" finds the shop on Google, sees the range and prices, and taps to order on WhatsApp. The owner spends time on buyers who already want to buy, not on answering "how much?" thirty times a day.

Quick replies worth setting up in WhatsApp Business

  • Prices: a short message with a link to the price list or product page on your website
  • Delivery: areas covered, costs and timeframes
  • Payment: accepted methods (EcoCash, OneMoney, card, cash on delivery) and how to pay
  • Location and hours: your address, a Google Maps link and opening times
  • After-hours: an away message that says when you'll reply and links to your website

Common mistakes

  • No WhatsApp button on the website, or one hidden at the bottom of the contact page.
  • Different prices on WhatsApp and the website, which confuses customers and costs trust.
  • "DM for price" everywhere. Many buyers won't ask. Publishing prices, or at least ranges, brings in more serious enquiries.
  • Slow replies. A website sends you more messages, so set up quick replies and away messages.

Is Your Website POTRAZ-Ready? A Data Protection Checklist

General information, not legal advice. This article explains how Zimbabwe's data protection rules affect business websites in practical terms. For decisions about licensing and legal obligations, speak to a qualified lawyer or contact POTRAZ.

Short answer: if your website, contact form, newsletter or customer list collects personal data (names, phone numbers, email addresses), Zimbabwe's Cyber and Data Protection Act applies to you. Under Statutory Instrument 155 of 2024, organisations processing personal data of 50 or more people generally need a data controller licence from POTRAZ, and POTRAZ announced mandatory compliance inspections from 1 September 2026. For your website, start with a privacy policy, consent on your forms, a secure HTTPS site and a clear record of where data is stored.

Data protection used to feel like something only banks and telecoms worried about. Not any more. A simple "Contact us" form or a WhatsApp list of customers is personal data, and it brings small businesses, schools, churches and NGOs into scope.

What the rules say (in plain English)

  • The law: the Cyber and Data Protection Act (Chapter 12:07) sets out how personal information must be collected, used, stored and protected in Zimbabwe.
  • The regulations: Statutory Instrument 155 of 2024 (the Cyber and Data Protection (Licensing of Data Controllers and Appointment of Data Protection Officers) Regulations) sets out licensing and the appointment of data protection officers.
  • Who needs a licence: organisations that process personal data of 50 or more individuals generally need a data controller licence from POTRAZ, which acts as the Data Protection Authority. Licence tiers and fees depend on how many people's data you process.
  • Data Protection Officer: licensed controllers are expected to appoint a data protection officer.
  • Inspections: POTRAZ issued a regulatory notice announcing mandatory, risk-based compliance inspections of data controllers from 1 September 2026.
  • Breaches: reported guidance says breaches must be reported to POTRAZ quickly, within 24 hours, and affected people notified within 72 hours.

Fees, tiers and deadlines can change. Check the current regulations and POTRAZ notices, and take legal advice for your specific situation.

Does my website collect personal data?

Probably. Personal data is any information that identifies a person. On a typical business website that includes:

  • Contact and enquiry forms: names, phone numbers, email addresses, messages
  • Newsletter sign-ups: email addresses
  • Online shops and bookings: names, addresses, phone numbers, order history
  • Job application forms: CVs and personal details
  • Analytics and cookies: IP addresses and device information
  • Linked WhatsApp lists and customer databases your website feeds into

If your organisation holds this kind of information about 50 or more people, the licensing rules are likely to apply.

Your website data protection checklist

1. Publish a privacy policy

A clear, plain-English page explaining what data you collect, why, how long you keep it, who you share it with, and how people can ask to see or delete their data. Link it in your website footer and next to every form.

2. Get consent on your forms

Add a short line and an unticked checkbox to forms: "I agree that [your business] may use my details to respond to my enquiry, as described in the privacy policy." For newsletters, ask for separate, explicit consent.

3. Collect only what you need

If a phone number is enough, don't ask for an ID number or home address. Less data means less risk.

4. Secure the website with HTTPS

Your site should load with https:// and a padlock. It encrypts form submissions between the visitor and your server.

5. Know where the data is stored

Do form submissions go to an email inbox, a database, a spreadsheet or a third-party service? Where is the server? Who has access? Write it down. Inspectors and your DPO will ask.

6. Limit access and keep software updated

Give staff access only to the data they need. Keep your website, plugins and content manager updated, and use strong passwords with two-step verification.

7. Have a breach plan

Decide who does what if data is leaked or a site is hacked, including who notifies POTRAZ and affected people, and how quickly.

8. Check your suppliers

Your web host, email provider, payment gateway and marketing tools all handle your customers' data. Make sure they're reputable and that your contracts cover data protection.

Example: consent wording for your contact form

Place this directly above your form's submit button, with an unticked checkbox:

☐ I agree that [Business name] may use the details I've provided to respond to my enquiry, as explained in our [Privacy Policy]. I can ask for my details to be deleted at any time.

For a newsletter, use a separate checkbox:

☐ Yes, I'd like to receive occasional emails with news and offers. I can unsubscribe at any time.

What to include in a website privacy policy

Keep it in plain English. A typical privacy policy covers:

  1. Who you are and how to contact you (and your Data Protection Officer, if appointed)
  2. What data you collect, such as names, phone numbers, emails, order details and website analytics
  3. Why you collect it: to respond to enquiries, deliver orders or send newsletters with consent
  4. Who you share it with, such as your web host, payment gateway or delivery partners
  5. How long you keep it, and how it is stored securely
  6. People's rights: to access, correct or delete their data, and how to ask
  7. Cookies and analytics used on the website
  8. Updates to the policy, with the date it was last changed

Have a lawyer review the final wording, especially if you handle sensitive data such as health, financial or children's information.

Agency vs Freelancer vs DIY: Who Should Build Your Website?

Short answer: DIY website builders (Wix, WordPress.com, AI site builders) are the cheapest in cash but cost you time and skills. A freelancer can deliver excellent work at a lower price, as long as there's a written contract and a plan for aftercare. An agency costs more but gives you a team, process and ongoing support. Whoever you choose, ask three things before paying: who owns the domain, is the price in writing, and who fixes the site after launch?

There's no single right answer. It depends on your budget, your time, how important the website is to your sales, and how much support you need afterwards. Here's an honest comparison.

Option 1: Do it yourself

How it works: you sign up for a website builder, choose a template, and add your own text and photos. Many now use AI to generate a first draft.

Pros - Lowest cash cost to get started - Full control; change things whenever you like - Fast to launch a simple site

Cons - It takes your time, often far more than expected - Design, writing, photos and SEO all depend on your skills - Local needs such as a .co.zw domain, EcoCash/Paynow payments and data protection basics can be harder to set up - Monthly platform fees add up, and moving off the platform later can be difficult

Best for: very small budgets, simple one-page sites, or testing an idea before investing more.

Option 2: Hire a freelancer

How it works: an independent designer or developer builds your site, often found through referrals, social media or freelance platforms.

Pros - Usually lower prices than an agency - Direct contact with the person doing the work - Many freelancers are highly skilled

Cons - One person means limited capacity: illness, travel or a full workload can delay your project - Quality and reliability vary widely, so references matter - Aftercare can be uncertain; some freelancers move on to other work or jobs - The classic risk: the designer "disappears" with the deposit, or controls the domain

Best for: clearly defined projects with a modest budget, where you've seen the freelancer's past work and agreed everything in writing.

Option 3: Work with an agency

How it works: a company with a team (designers, developers, writers, marketers) plans and builds your site and usually offers support afterwards.

Pros - A team with a range of skills: design, development, content, SEO, ads - Defined process, timelines and project management - Continuity: if one person leaves, the company is still there - Aftercare and support plans, plus help with marketing once you're live

Cons - Higher price than most freelancers or DIY - You may deal with an account manager rather than the person writing the code

Best for: businesses whose website is central to winning customers, organisations with procurement and compliance needs (corporates, NGOs, law firms, schools), and anyone who wants ongoing help with SEO and marketing.

The 3 questions to ask before you pay anyone

1. Who owns the domain?

The domain (yourbusiness.co.zw) must be registered in your name or your company's name. If it's in the builder's name, you don't fully control your online address.

2. Is the price in writing?

Get a written quote listing pages, features, timeline, payment schedule and yearly costs. It protects you from surprise invoices. See 5 signs you're overpaying for your website.

3. Who fixes it after launch?

Websites need updates, backups and occasional fixes. Agree who handles them, how quickly, and at what cost, before the project starts. Ask whether you'll get a content manager so you can make simple changes yourself.

What about AI website builders?

AI site builders can produce a decent-looking first draft in minutes, which is genuinely useful for testing an idea. But the words are generic, the structure may not match how Zimbabwean customers search, and local essentials still need a human: a .co.zw domain, EcoCash or Paynow payments, a Google Business Profile and data protection basics. Treat AI as a fast starting point, not a finished website.

More smart questions

  • Can I see three websites you've built, and speak to those clients?
  • Will the site be fast on mobile data?
  • Is basic SEO and a Google Business Profile included?
  • Will I get logins for everything: domain, hosting, website admin?
  • What happens to my site if we stop working together?

Red flags in any website quote

Whether it comes from a freelancer or an agency, be careful if you see:

  • No written quote, only a price on WhatsApp
  • 100% payment upfront for a large project, with no milestones
  • The domain registered in the builder's name
  • No examples of past work, or no clients you can speak to
  • Vague scope, such as "a professional website" with no list of pages or features
  • No mention of aftercare: who fixes problems, and at what cost
  • Promises of "guaranteed first place on Google". Nobody can guarantee rankings.

A good provider will be happy to put everything in writing, show you their work and explain what happens after launch.

Frequently asked questions

Should I hire an agency or a freelancer for my website?

Either can work. Choose a freelancer for smaller, clearly defined projects where you trust their track record; choose an agency when the website is central to your sales or you need a team, process and support.

How do I know a web designer won't disappear with my deposit?

Check references and past work, sign a written agreement, pay in stages tied to milestones, and make sure the domain and hosting are in your name.

Who owns my website and domain after it's built?

You should. Put ownership of the domain, content and design files in your contract, and make sure you receive all logins.

Is Wix or WordPress good enough for a small business in Zimbabwe?

They can be, especially for simple sites. Check that local needs such as a .co.zw domain, EcoCash/Paynow payments and mobile speed are covered.

How long does it take to build a website?

Simple sites can launch in days; larger sites with payments or many pages take weeks. A written timeline is part of a good quote.

Can I start with DIY and move to an agency later?

Yes, many businesses do. Keep the domain in your own name from day one, keep copies of your text and photos, and choose a platform that lets you export your content. That makes it much easier to rebuild or move your site when you're ready to invest more.
